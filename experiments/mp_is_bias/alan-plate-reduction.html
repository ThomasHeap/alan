The question

When a plate's variable shares one K-dim, what does "shared" mean?

Say theta lives inside a plate of size 2 (two observations, theta_1 and theta_2), each conditioned on a shared parent mu, and alan draws K particles for theta — one K-dimension, not one per plate element. That's the efficient part of the design: you don't pay for K per replicate. But it leaves a real question open: when replicate 1 and replicate 2 each need "a theta value," do they have to agree on which of the K particles they're using, or can each replicate pick independently?

I built a white-box reimplementation of this reduction to test something else entirely (a jackknife bias correction) and got answers that didn't match alan's own output. Chasing that down landed here: I'd silently assumed the first (shared, lockstep) answer. alan implements the second. They're not approximations of each other — they're different sums, and only one of them is the exact answer the papers' math actually specifies.

Two candidate answers

Lockstep vs. independent

Wrong — what I assumed

Order A — one shared index

For a fixed particle k, both replicates use theta_1[k] and theta_2[k] together, as a single two-element hypothesis. Sum each hypothesis's log-density across the two replicates first, then normalize once over the K shared hypotheses.

logsumexpk [ Σi fi(k) ]
Correct — what alan does

Order B — independent per replicate

Each replicate independently asks "which of the K particles best explains just me?" — normalize (logsumexp) over K separately per replicate, then sum those two already-reduced numbers across the plate.

Σi logsumexpk [ fi(k) ]

logsumexp doesn't distribute over an inner sum — logΣexp(a+b) ≠ logΣexp(a) + logΣexp(b) in general — so these aren't two ways of writing the same computation. They give different numbers. The next section shows exactly how different, and which one is actually right.

Ground truth: enumerate all K³=8 combinations directly

Since mu, theta_1, and theta_2 are three separate latent "nodes" in the graph (they're conditionally independent of each other given their parents), the literal, no-shortcuts answer sums the importance weight over every combination of (k_mu, k_theta1, k_theta2) — 2×2×2 = 8 terms, each one a full unnormalized importance weight for one complete hypothesis:

total = Σ_{kmu,k1,k2}  p(mu[kmu]) × [p(theta1[k1]|mu[kmu])/q(theta1[k1])] × [p(theta2[k2]|mu[kmu])/q(theta2[k2])]
                          × p(x1|theta1[k1]) × p(x2|theta2[k2])

total = 4.594198

Order A doesn't just round differently — it's missing more than half the true total. It only ever considers the two "diagonal" hypotheses (both replicates using particle 0, or both using particle 1) and throws away the two "off-diagonal" ones (replicate 1 using particle 0 while replicate 2 uses particle 1, and vice versa) — exactly the terms a shared, lockstep index can never reach.

Why B is exact, not just "a reasonable alternative"

A sum over independent products factors into a product of sums

This isn't a design choice with a tradeoff attached — Order B is the only one of the two that computes the real answer, and it falls straight out of one algebra fact. Given mu, the replicates are conditionally independent: theta_1 and theta_2 don't interact except through their shared parent. So the joint importance weight for one full assignment (k_mu, k_1, k_2) factors into a product of per-replicate terms, and summing a product of independent terms over independent indices distributes:

Σ_{k1,k2}  f₁(k1) · f₂(k2)   =   [ Σ_k1 f₁(k1) ]  ·  [ Σ_k2 f₂(k2) ]

Take logs of both sides and the product of two independent sums becomes a sum of two independent logsumexps — exactly Order B. This is the same distributive-law trick that makes the whole massively-parallel method tractable at the level of separate latent variables (the Kn → polynomial-time story); it turns out to apply again, one level down, inside a single plate. A plate of size P effectively reasons over KP combinations of "who used which particle," computed exactly, in O(P·K) work — not because of an approximation, but because conditional independence given the shared parent makes that factorization lossless.

Ties together

This is the same mechanism, one level more granular, as Group.py's own documented reason for grouping variables (turning K³ into K² by sharing a K-dim across correlated siblings) and as the general "why strict plate nesting" story for the whole library (each plate boundary is exactly where a local, exact reduction over conditionally-independent structure happens). Order A would have thrown away most of that power for any plate of size > 1 — not a subtle loss, a 51% one even at P=2.

Where this lives

Two lines, `reduce_Ks` and `_logPQ_plate`

The plate dimension stays a live, broadcast axis all the way through reduce_Ks — confirmed directly: build a toy 2-element-plate × 3-particle tensor, hand it to reduce_Ks, and the result still carries the plate dim. The actual summation across the plate is a separate, later step:

# src/alan/logpq.py, _logPQ_plate
lp = reduce_Ks(lps, all_Ks)      # reduces the K-dim; plate dim still live, broadcast
...
lp = lp.sum(new_platedim)        # THEN sums across the plate -- on already-log-reduced values

That ordering — reduce the K-dim while the plate dim is still open, sum the plate dim only afterward — is the entire mechanism. Nothing else needs to change per-plate; it's a structural consequence of when in the recursion the plate dimension gets closed out.

What changes downstream

Redone against Order B, the jackknife finding gets more interesting, not less

The original jackknife experiment (three delete-one variants, none helped) was run entirely against the wrong reduction, so it's been rebuilt on Order B. The two K-dims now play structurally different roles — mu only ever enters a flat, linear ratio-of-sums at the root (the classical self-normalized-IS shape the jackknife theory was built for), while theta enters through a per-plate-element logsumexp, a fundamentally different kind of reduction. That structural difference shows up directly in the results:

Jackknifing mu (the flat, linear ratio) now shows a real effect, clearest exactly where classical SNIS bias theory says it should show up most: at K=3, bias drops more than 10× (−0.047 → −0.004), fading to a no-op by K≈30 as the underlying bias itself becomes small relative to Monte Carlo noise. Jackknifing theta (the per-element logsumexp) never helps, at any K — consistent with the classical jackknife's bias-cancellation algebra being derived for flat self-normalized ratios, which the inner reduction simply isn't.
